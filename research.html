Four questions, one set of tools.

Everything below rests on the same bet: that the statistical behaviour of a microbial community can be captured by models with very few moving parts, and that the places where those models break are where the biology is hiding.

01 / Laws

Macroecological patterns and the models that generate them

Abundance distributions, the scaling of variance with mean, the relationship between occupancy and abundance — the same handful of patterns recur across soils, oceans, hosts and bioreactors. We ask what minimal process can produce all of them at once. A stochastic logistic model of growth, with environmental noise and a carrying capacity, turns out to reproduce a surprising amount of it, which raises the more interesting question of what it cannot do.

Related work: eLife 2024, Nat. Ecol. Evol. 2017, a survey and outlook.

02 / Experiments

Putting the laws under experimental stress

Patterns observed in survey data are easy to explain and hard to falsify. Experimental communities let us do better: we can fix the environment, replicate it, perturb it, and then ask whether the statistical regularities hold, shift, or disappear. Recent work has looked at how a balance between environmental filtering and competitive exclusion shapes alternative stable states, and at what survives when a community is assembled from scratch.

Related work: PLOS Comput. Biol. 2025, preprint 2025.

03 / Life history

Dormancy, energy limitation and the cost of waiting

Most microbial cells, most of the time, are not growing. Dormancy and sporulation create a seed bank that buffers populations against bad conditions — and in doing so they slow evolution, change which mutations fix, and alter how a lineage responds to selection. We study the bioenergetics of these strategies and their consequences for population genetics, including what happens under energy limitation severe enough that maintenance, not growth, sets the pace.

Related work: PNAS 2026, Mol. Biol. Evol. 2021, Evol. Appl. 2018.

04 / Evolution in situ

Strains, sojourns and evolution inside a host

The human gut is an evolution experiment nobody designed, running in billions of replicates. Dense time series let us watch strains rise and fall, ask how long a lineage persists once it arrives, and test whether ecological stability at the species level is really stability at the level of strains. The same macroecological language applies, one level down.

Related work: mSystems 2026, mBio 2023, GBE 2022.
